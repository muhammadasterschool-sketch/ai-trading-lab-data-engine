1. Introduction to Trading
Trading is the act of buying and selling financial instruments with the goal of generating profit from price
movements. Unlike long-term investing, which focuses on holding assets for years, trading typically operates on
shorter time horizons—ranging from seconds to several months.
At its core, trading rests on a simple idea: buy low and sell high (or sell high and buy low when shorting).
Success depends on analysis, discipline, risk control, and emotional management more than on any single
indicator or “secret” system.

Key Participants
• Retail traders — Individual traders using personal capital.
• Institutional traders — Banks, hedge funds, mutual funds, and prop firms.
• Market makers — Firms that provide liquidity by continuously quoting bid and ask prices.
• Brokers — Intermediaries that execute orders on behalf of clients.

3. Essential Market Concepts
Bull vs Bear Market
A bull market is characterized by rising prices and optimism. A bear market features falling prices and
pessimism. These terms also describe individual assets or sectors.

Long vs Short
Going long means buying an asset expecting its price to rise. Going short means selling (or borrowing to sell)
an asset expecting its price to fall, then buying it back cheaper.

Bid, Ask & Spread

The bid is the highest price a buyer is willing to pay. The ask (or offer) is the lowest price a seller will accept.
The difference is the spread — a key cost of trading.

Liquidity
How easily an asset can be bought or sold without significantly affecting its price. High liquidity = tight spreads
and easy execution.

Volatility
The degree of price fluctuation. High volatility offers larger potential moves (and larger risk). Measured by
indicators such as ATR or standard deviation.

Leverage & Margin
Leverage allows control of a larger position with less capital. Margin is the collateral required. Leverage
amplifies both gains and losses.

Volume
The number of units traded in a period. Rising volume often confirms the strength of a price move.

Slippage
The difference between the expected fill price and the actual fill price, especially in fast or illiquid markets.

5. Technical Analysis
Technical analysis studies price action and volume to forecast future movements. It assumes that all available
information is already reflected in price, and that price tends to move in trends and patterns.

Chart Types
• Line chart — Simple close-to-close line; good for overall trend.
• Bar chart (OHLC) — Shows Open, High, Low, Close for each period.

• Heikin-Ashi — Modified candles that smooth noise and highlight trend.

Core Concepts
Support & Resistance — Support is a price level where buying interest tends to appear; resistance is where
selling pressure emerges. These levels can reverse roles once broken.
Trend — An uptrend shows higher highs and higher lows; a downtrend shows lower highs and lower lows.
Sideways / ranging markets move within a horizontal channel.
Chart Patterns — Classic formations such as Head & Shoulders, Double Top/Bottom, Triangles, Flags, and
Wedges that traders use to anticipate continuation or reversal.

6. Fundamental Analysis
Fundamental analysis evaluates the intrinsic value of an asset by examining economic, financial, and qualitative
factors. It answers “why” price might move, while technical analysis focuses on “when.”

For Stocks
• Financial statements: Income statement, balance sheet, cash-flow statement.
• Ratios: P/E, PEG, P/B, ROE, debt-to-equity, free-cash-flow yield.
• Qualitative: Management quality, competitive moat, industry trends, regulation.
• Earnings reports and guidance are major catalysts.

For Forex & Macro
• Interest rates and central-bank policy (Fed, ECB, BOJ, etc.).
• Inflation data (CPI, PPI), employment reports, GDP growth.
• Geopolitical events, trade balances, and risk sentiment (risk-on / risk-off).

For Commodities
• Supply shocks, weather, inventories, OPEC decisions, industrial demand.

8. Risk Management
Risk management is widely regarded as the most important skill in trading. Even a strategy with a positive edge
will eventually fail without proper risk controls.

Core Principles
• Position sizing — Never risk more than a small percentage of capital on a single trade (commonly
0.5–2%). Calculate size from the distance to your stop-loss.
• Risk-Reward ratio — Aim for setups where potential reward is at least 1.5–2× the amount risked. A 1:2
ratio means you can be right only 40% of the time and still be profitable.
• Stop-loss discipline — Define the invalidation point before entry and honor it. Moving stops further away
is a common path to large losses.
• Diversification & correlation — Avoid concentrating risk in highly correlated instruments.
• Maximum drawdown limits — Set a personal rule (e.g., stop trading for the day/week after a certain %
loss) to prevent emotional spirals.
• Leverage awareness — Higher leverage increases the probability of ruin even with a good strategy.
Example: With a $10,000 account and 1% risk rule, maximum loss per trade = $100. If your stop is 50 pips
away, your position size must be set so that 50 pips = $100.

9. Trading Psychology
Emotional control often separates consistent traders from the rest. Markets trigger fear, greed, hope, and
regret—biases that lead to poor decisions.

Common Psychological Pitfalls
• FOMO (Fear Of Missing Out) — Entering late because price is already moving.
• Revenge trading — Immediately trying to recover a loss with a larger or impulsive trade.

• Overconfidence after wins — Increasing size or ignoring rules after a streak.
• Analysis paralysis — Overloading charts with indicators until no clear decision is possible.
• Confirmation bias — Seeking only information that supports an existing view.
• Loss aversion — Holding losers too long and cutting winners too early.

Helpful Practices
• Keep a trading journal (setup, emotion, outcome, lessons).
• Pre-define rules and follow a written trading plan.
• Accept that losses are a normal cost of doing business.
• Take breaks after emotional sessions; protect mental capital.
• Focus on process and execution quality more than individual P&L.

10. Common Trading Strategies
A strategy is a repeatable set of rules for entry, exit, and risk. Below are widely discussed approaches (none is
guaranteed to be profitable).

Trend Following
Identify and trade in the direction of the prevailing trend using moving averages, breakouts, or
higher-highs/higher-lows. Works best in strongly trending markets; suffers in choppy conditions.

Mean Reversion
Assume price will return to an average after extreme moves. Often uses RSI, Bollinger Bands, or
support/resistance. Works better in ranging markets.

Breakout Trading
Enter when price breaks a significant support/resistance level or chart pattern with increased volume. False
breakouts are common; confirmation and stop placement are critical.

Pullback / Retracement
In an established trend, wait for a temporary counter-move (pullback) to a moving average or Fibonacci level,
then enter in the direction of the trend.

Range Trading
Buy near support and sell near resistance inside a well-defined horizontal range. Avoid when a breakout is
imminent.

News / Event Trading
Trade around scheduled economic releases or earnings. High volatility and widened spreads; requires
experience and strict risk limits.

Scalping Micro-Structure
Exploit small inefficiencies and order-flow imbalances on very short timeframes. Demands low latency, tight
spreads, and intense focus.

12. Final Notes & Best Practices
Trading is a probabilistic activity. Even the best strategies experience losing streaks. Long-term survival and
profitability depend more on risk management, emotional discipline, and continuous learning than on finding a
“perfect” indicator.

Practical Checklist for New Traders
• Educate yourself thoroughly before risking real capital.
• Start with a demo account or very small size.
• Write a trading plan: markets, style, setups, risk rules, review process.
• Keep a detailed journal and review it regularly.
• Protect capital first — focus on not blowing up the account.
• Treat trading as a business, not entertainment or gambling.
• Be realistic about returns; high advertised returns usually imply high risk or are unrealistic.
• Continuously refine your process; markets evolve.
